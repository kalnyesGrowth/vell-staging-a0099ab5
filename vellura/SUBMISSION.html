#1 — Female facial hair

Vertical: what problem

Unwanted coarse facial hair on the lip, chin and jawline in women. Unsexy, appearance-driven,
chronic, and already monetised — which is your own filter, three for three.

The daily behaviour is the product opportunity. She is not looking for a cure. She is
managing something on a two-to-three day clock: tweezers in the bag, the car mirror because
bathroom light lies, checking her jaw before a meeting. That is a maintenance habit with a
subscription shape already built into it.

Market: proof people pay

They already pay, just not to us. Waxing and threading run $30–60 a session on a 2–4 week
cycle. Laser runs $200–400 a session across 6–8 sessions and does not hold against
hormonal regrowth, which is precisely the PCOS-adjacent population we are selling to.
Electrolysis is the only permanent answer and costs $1,500–4,500 over years.

So the money is being spent. It is being spent on methods that cannot slow growth, by
someone who has usually already paid for at least one of them and been disappointed.

That last part matters more than market size. Per your own read on telehealth buyers: most
are repeat category buyers, and the creative has to answer why this works when the thing
you already bought did not. Here that answer is unusually clean, and it is not a
positioning trick — every method she has tried acts on the hair, and none of them act on
the follicle.

Economics: does it work at $150 CAC

The correction first, because the version I sent you was wrong. Every payback table in
the earlier draft was built per survivor and presented as if it were per acquisition.
It showed what a customer contributes in M1 and M2 if she is still there, on the same page
where I accepted your 60 to 80% monthly churn assumption. Both cannot be true. A table that
says payback lands at M1 while most of the cohort has already left is not a payback table, it
is a description of the people who stayed. Everything below is the same business modelled per
acquisition, which is the only version that tells you whether the $150 comes back.

The cost stack, stated once so it can be checked. At the FP71 rate of $1.75 a gram, 20g

The same model as a function of price, because the price is not settled either. $119 is
the base case, not a signed number, so here is the model with the price left as a variable.
At a monthly list price P and monthly survival s:

per-acquisition contribution = (P + 25 − 35 − 8 − 25 − 7 − 150) + (P − 50) × s/(1−s)
                             = (P − 200) + (P − 50) × s/(1−s)

v2 proposals, not current design

Three things I would put on the roadmap rather than in the build. None of these is in the
Figma package and none is priced into the model above. They are here because the price
correction surfaced them and because two of them attack the parts of this model I am least
comfortable defending.

1. Area-based dosing tiers instead of one size. Lip only 20g, lip and chin 40g, lip and
chin and jawline 60g. Q-02 already collects the area data that would set the dose, so the
intake needs no new question and the clinician gets a dose that matches what she actually
told us she is treating. The strategic point is not the revenue, it is that it takes the
product out of the competitor's unit of sale. Right now Oana publishes a 60g at $139 and we
publish 20g at $119, which means anyone can put the two on a per-gram basis and we lose that
comparison. Dosing by area makes the unit of sale clinical rather than commercial, and a
clinical unit cannot be undercut per gram because it is not the same object. This is the
cleanest answer I have to the parity problem in the competitor section, and it is a v2 change
because it needs the clinician network to agree that area maps to dose.

2. Re-quote the 3-active across two or three other pharmacies in the catalog. $1.75 a
gram is 2 to 2.6 times above this venture's own priced dermatology band of $0.68 to $0.92 a
gram ($20.50 to $27.50 per 30g). That gap is either a real cost of the three-active
combination or it is one pharmacy's number, and we do not currently know which. Every
threshold in the model above moves with it, so this is the cheapest possible piece of work
with the largest effect on the verdict. It should happen before the $8,500 does.

3. Spanish-first as a CAC thesis, not just a nice-to-have lane. Everything else on this
page defends margin. Spanish is the only lever on the table that attacks acquisition cost,
which is the other half of the equation and the half no repricing can reach. The thesis is an
uncontested auction: the search and social demand exists in Spanish while no prescription

  7. Metformin is the weak link in the story. Thin human evidence topically. Currently
